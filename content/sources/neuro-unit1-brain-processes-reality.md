**Unit 1: How Your Brain Processes Reality**

1. # **Brain Structure & Function**

The human brain is a highly complex organ composed of many specialized regions, each playing a vital role in perception, emotion, memory, and behavior. Among these, the **prefrontal cortex** is key for decision-making, planning, and personality expression. This region, located at the front of the brain, helps us evaluate situations, consider future consequences, and control our impulses. The **temporal lobe**, located on the sides of the brain, is responsible for auditory processing and memory formation. Together, these regions work with others such as the **occipital lobe** (for vision) and the **parietal lobe** (for spatial awareness) to allow us to interpret the world around us.

**Perception** is the process by which our brain interprets sensory information, and this wouldn’t be possible without specialized brain areas. The occipital lobe decodes visual input, while the temporal lobe handles sounds. The **somatosensory cortex**, located in the parietal lobe, receives touch-related data, and the insular cortex is believed to play a role in taste and internal body states. These areas communicate constantly to give us a seamless understanding of our surroundings. Damage to even one of these areas can lead to significant disruptions in how we perceive the world, such as blindness, deafness, etc.

The brain’s structure is supported by deeper areas such as the **limbic system** and **brainstem**. The **hippocampus**, for example, is essential for turning short-term memories into long-term ones, while the amygdala is important for emotional reactions, particularly fear. The **brainstem** controls basic life functions like breathing and heart rate, making it critical for survival. Understanding how these regions interact gives scientists and students insight into disorders, brain injuries, and how learning and behavior are shaped.

## Key Terms & Facts

1. **Prefrontal Cortex**: Region involved in decision-making and personality.  
2. **Temporal Lobe**: Processes auditory information and memory.  
3. **Occipital Lobe**: Interprets visual information.  
4. **Parietal Lobe**: Manages spatial awareness and touch.  
5. **Somatosensory Cortex**: Area that processes touch and physical sensations.  
6. **Limbic System**: Set of brain structures involved in emotions and memory.  
7. **Hippocampus**: Brain structure involved in memory formation.  
8. **Amygdala**: Controls emotional responses like fear.  
9. **Brainstem**: Regulates basic life-sustaining functions.  
10. **Perception**: The process of organizing and interpreting sensory information.

## Important Statistics

1. The human brain has around 86 billion neurons.  
2. The prefrontal cortex is not fully developed until around age 25\.  
3. The brain uses about 20% of the body's total energy.  
4. The hippocampus can shrink due to chronic stress.  
5. Damage to the occipital lobe can cause visual hallucinations or blindness.

2. # **Sensory Processing**

**Sensory processing** is the brain’s ability to receive, organize, and interpret sensory input from the environment. Humans have five primary senses, sight, sound, touch, taste, and smell. And each one is processed by specific parts of the brain. For example, vision is processed in the occipital lobe, where signals from the eyes are interpreted as shapes, colors, and motion. Auditory information travels to the temporal lobe, while the **somatosensory cortex** manages the sense of touch, including pressure and pain. The **gustatory cortex** interprets taste, and the olfactory bulb helps identify and process smells.

Integration of senses is crucial for coherent perception. The brain combines input from multiple senses to form a complete picture of the environment. This process, called **multisensory integration**, allows us to connect sounds with images or smells with tastes. The brain’s **thalamus** plays a central role in this process by acting as a relay station, filtering and directing sensory data to the right areas. For instance, seeing someone clap and hearing the sound at the same time helps us understand what’s happening and react appropriately. Without this coordination, our perception would feel confusing or disconnected.

Problems with sensory processing can lead to conditions like S**ensory Processing Disorder** (SPD), where the brain has difficulty receiving and responding to information. Children with SPD may be overly sensitive to sound or touch, or they may not respond to sensory input at all. Sensory challenges are also common in individuals with autism spectrum disorder. Understanding how the brain processes senses can help educators, caregivers, and therapists develop better ways to support those with sensory difficulties. Strategies like sensory-friendly classrooms, occupational therapy, and mindfulness exercises can be used to improve a person's ability to engage with their environment.

## Key Terms & Facts

1. **Sensory Processing**: The way the brain interprets sensory input.  
2. **Multisensory Integration**: Combining information from multiple senses.  
3. **Thalamus**: The brain's sensory relay station.  
4. **Somatosensory Cortex:** Processes touch-related signals.  
5. **Visual Cortex:** Part of the brain that processes visual information.  
6. **Auditory Cortex**: Interprets sounds and language.  
7. **Sensory Processing Disorder (SPD)**: A condition where sensory input is misinterpreted.  
8. **Neuroplasticity**: The brain’s ability to change and adapt over time.  
9. **Stimulus**: Any external input that activates the senses.  
10. **Perception**: The experience created by sensory processing.

## Important Statistics

1. 1 in 6 children have sensory processing challenges.  
2. Vision accounts for over 80% of the information our brain receives.  
3. The thalamus handles around 98% of all sensory input.  
4. Touch sensitivity affects up to 95% of children with autism.  
5. The human nose can detect over 1 trillion different scents.

3. # **Cognitive Basics**

**Cognitive biases** are systematic errors in thinking that affect how we make decisions and interpret information. These mental shortcuts happen automatically and are often useful for quick judgments. However, they can also lead to errors. For example, **confirmation bias** is the tendency to focus on information that agrees with what we already believe while ignoring facts that challenge our views. This can lead to tunnel vision, especially in environments where people are frequently exposed to similar perspectives, like on social media. Another bias, the **availability heuristic**, causes people to overestimate the likelihood of an event based on how easily examples come to mind, rather than on real data.

These biases impact our everyday lives, from school projects to social interactions and even professional decisions. For instance, someone using the availability heuristic might assume that shark attacks are common just because they recently saw a news report about one, even though such attacks are rare. Confirmation bias might make a student ignore peer-reviewed sources when doing research if the sources don’t match their opinion. Such biases can affect judgment in important areas like healthcare, law, hiring practices, and public policy, which is why learning to spot them is essential.

Becoming aware of cognitive biases helps individuals think more critically and make better decisions. Teachers can encourage students to examine different perspectives and use evidence-based reasoning. Professionals in law, medicine, and business benefit from recognizing their own biases when interpreting data or interacting with clients. Ultimately, reducing the influence of cognitive biases improves fairness, creativity, and clarity in both personal and group decision-making.

## Key Terms & Facts

1. **Cognitive Bias**: A systematic pattern of deviation from rationality.  
2. **Confirmation Bias:** Favoring information that confirms preexisting beliefs.  
3. **Availability Heuristic:** Judging events based on how easily examples come to mind.  
4. **Heuristic**: A mental shortcut used to make decisions quickly.  
5. **Anchoring Bia**s: Relying too heavily on the first piece of information.  
6. **Dunning-Kruger Effect**: When people overestimate their own knowledge.  
7. **Framing Effect**: Decisions influenced by how information is presented.  
8. **Stereotyping**: Assuming characteristics based on group membership.  
9. **Implicit Bias**: Attitudes or stereotypes that affect understanding subconsciously.  
10. **Rational Thinking**: Making decisions based on logic and evidence.

## Important Statistics

1. Over 180 cognitive biases have been identified by psychologists.  
2. 70% of people show confirmation bias in online searches.  
3. The Dunning-Kruger Effect is most common in people with the least expertise.  
4. Anchoring can change decision outcomes by as much as 50%.  
5. In mock trials, framing a case as "loss" vs. "gain" changed jury verdicts by 30%.
