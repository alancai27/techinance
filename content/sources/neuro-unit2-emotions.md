**Unit 2: The Neuroscience Behind Emotions**

1. # **Emotional Brain Networks**

The emotional brain owes its complexities to the intricate collaboration among core structures: the amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex. The **amygdala** is central to our immediate emotional reactions, especially in assessing threats. It swiftly interprets sensory information, often before our conscious mind even registers it, resulting in rapid emotional responses like fear or anxiety. Its outputs then activate related systems including those governing physiological arousal, memory, and motor action, enabling the body to respond to perceived threats in real time.

Complementing the amygdala’s alarm system, the **hippocampus** weaves emotional context into our experiences. It encodes details of events, like time, place, and environmental cues, so that emotional memories can guide future behavior. For instance, remembering a past encounter with a barking dog can trigger caution the next time a dog is nearby. The hippocampus thereby ensures that emotion isn't just reactive; it's informed by memory. This memory-based grounding helps us interpret present cues with the nuanced backdrop of past experience, allowing emotional responses to be more accurate and adaptive.

Meanwhile, the **prefrontal cortex (PFC)** acts as an emotional regulator and decision-maker. Located in the brain’s frontal lobe, it oversees complex processes such as planning, evaluation, inhibition, and modulation of emotional impulses. When facing a strong emotional stimulus, say feeling anger during a disagreement, the PFC can help us pause, consider consequences, and choose a thoughtful response over a reflexive one. Over time, through repeated engagement, the PFC becomes better at dampening excessive reactivity, enabling emotional maturity and resilience. Working in tandem, these brain regions modulate emotion both implicitly (amygdala-driven instinct) and explicitly (PFC-guided reasoning), creating a dynamic system that balances speed with deliberation.

## Key Terms & Facts

1. Amygdala: brain structure that processes fear and threat signals  
2. Hippocampus: region involved in forming and retrieving emotional memories  
3. Prefrontal Cortex: frontal brain area responsible for regulating emotions and impulse control  
4. Emotional Network:  interconnected brain regions involved in emotion generation  
5. Threat Detection:  rapid evaluation of potential danger by the amygdala  
6. Emotional Memory: memory encoded with emotional significance  
7. Top-down Control: cognitive regulation from higher-level brain areas  
8. Functional Connectivity: communication pathways between brain regions  
9. Limbic System: network of brain regions involved in emotion  
10. Emotional Regulation: modulation of emotional responses via neural control

## Important Statistics

1. Teenage emotional intensity is heightened; the amygdala matures earlier than the prefrontal cortex   
2. Individuals with high emotional intelligence show increased activity in the prefrontal cortex  
3. The uncinate fasciculus connects the amygdala and hippocampus to the orbitofrontal cortex, critical for emotional response modulation.  
4. The amygdala and prefrontal systems jointly support flexible emotion processing and executive control  
5. Emotions like anger involve unique activation patterns: e.g., right hippocampus, amygdala, and bilateral prefrontal cortex

2. # **Neurochemistry of Emotions**

Emotions are not just the product of circuits and firing rates—they're deeply entwined with neurochemistry. **Dopamine** plays an important role in reward processing and motivation. When we experience pleasure or engage in a goal, seeking behaviors, dopamine surges in the brain’s reward pathways, reinforcing those actions. This is why achieving a long-sought goal brings forward feelings of satisfaction.

In contrast, **serotonin** is often linked to mood stability and contentment. Lower levels of serotonin are associated with depressive and anxious states, while balanced serotonin is tied to emotional calm. It modulates how we interpret negative events, higher serotonin tends to make us more resilient. Meanwhile, **norepinephrine** primes the body for action during stress. It heightens attention, mobilizes energy, and readies us for fight-or-flight. Thus, sudden spikes in norepinephrine can make us feel alert or agitated, depending on context.

Beyond these, the interplay of **GABA** and **glutamate** is essential for emotional equilibrium. Glutamate excites neurons and is vital for learning, while GABA calms the nervous system, helping us relax. A healthy balance enables us to experience emotions without being overwhelmed. When glutamate dominates, we may feel restless or anxious; when GABA overshadows, cravings for lethargy or numbness may follow. Therefore, the nuanced orchestration of these neurochemicals shapes emotional tone, intensity, and flexibility.

## Key Terms & Facts

## Important Statistics

1. Exercise increases endorphins and boosts dopamine, improving mood  
2. Lowered serotonin levels are strongly linked to depression symptoms  
3. Dopamine dysregulation is implicated in addiction and schizophrenia  
4. Excessive glutamate (without GABA) can result in overstimulation/anxiety; too much GABA may cause lethargy/depression  
5. Emotional mapping shows norepinephrine linked to stress emotions like fear and anger in imaging studies

3. # **Emotional Regulation**

Emotional regulation relies on strategic cognitive and behavioral practices, steered by the brain’s regulatory systems. **Cognitive reappraisal**, for instance, involves reframing how we interpret emotionally charged situations, turning “I messed up” into “I learned from that.” Brain imaging shows that this technique consistently activates prefrontal regions (like the ventrolateral PFC), indicating that it works by engaging executive resources to modify emotional response.

**Mindfulness** centers on present-moment, nonjudgmental awareness of both thoughts and emotions. This practice enhances emotional regulation by allowing us to observe feelings without immediate reaction. Neurologically, mindfulness strengthens functional connections between prefrontal areas and emotion-generating regions like the amygdala, making it easier to notice rising emotion and respond thoughtfully rather than impulsively.

Other structured methods, **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)**, which encourages emotional openness, and **Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)**, which teaches skills such as distress tolerance and interpersonal effectiveness, harness specific neural mechanisms. ACT can boost activity in brain networks related to self-awareness and acceptance, while DBT enhances the capacity of prefrontal control regions to manage emotional arousal. Together, these approaches leverage the brain’s adaptability to reshape how we experience and cope with emotions.

## Key Terms & Facts

1. Emotional Regulation: altering which emotions we experience and how we express them  
2. Mindfulness: non-judgmental present-moment awareness technique  
3. Cognitive Reappraisal: changing interpretation to alter emotional response  
4. ACT (Acceptance & Commitment Therapy): therapeutic approach for emotional flexibility  
5. DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy): therapy focusing on regulating emotions and behavior  
6. Frontoparietal Control Network: brain circuit for executive regulation  
7. Self-distancing: viewing situations objectively to reduce emotional intensity  
8. Distraction: shifting attention away from emotion-triggering stimuli  
9. Emotion Dysregulation: inability to manage emotional responses adequately  
10. Neuroplasticity: brain’s ability to adapt through experience and training

## Important Statistics

1. Mindfulness and therapy practices can rewire emotional circuits over time  
2. Reappraisal shows the least increased brain activation as emotion intensity increases, indicating efficiency  
3. Reappraisal engages frontoparietal control regions more strongly than self-distancing or distraction  
4. Emotional regulation failures are central in developmental and psychopathological contexts  
5. Non-invasive imaging reliably identifies brain systems that support successful emotion regulation
